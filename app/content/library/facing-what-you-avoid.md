Avoiding something frightening brings instant relief, and the relief is exactly the problem: it teaches the mind that the danger was real and that avoiding it is what kept you safe. The fear gets a little stronger each time.

## Doing the opposite, in small pieces

Facing it works the other way round. You stay in contact with the thing long enough for the distress to come down on its own, and the mind learns something it cannot be told: that the feared outcome did not happen, or that it happened and you got through it.

## Small enough to actually do

The step should be one you are fairly sure you can complete — not the hardest version. Two floors in the lift, not twenty. A message to one person, not a party. Repeating an easy step until it is boring beats attempting a hard one once.

## Recording it

Note the distress before, during and after. The numbers usually do not drop on the first attempt, and the second and third are where the change shows.

## Where this belongs with a professional

Facing a past traumatic event is not this. That work needs a therapist who can be with you while you do it — not an app, and not alone at 2am.
